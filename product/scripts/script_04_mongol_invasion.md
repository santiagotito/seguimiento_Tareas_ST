# Script 04: How the Mongols Killed 40 Million People

---

## [HOOK — 0:00–0:45]

Ten percent.

That's how much of the entire human population on earth was killed by one man and the armies he unleashed.

In the 13th century, Genghis Khan and his successors killed an estimated 40 million people. In some regions they conquered, the population didn't recover for centuries.

But here's the part nobody tells you: they also created the largest empire in human history, enabled the first global trade network, and sparked a cultural exchange that helped end the Dark Ages.

The Mongols were simultaneously the most destructive and one of the most transformative forces in human history.

---

## [GENGHIS KHAN — 0:45–3:00]

His name at birth was Temüjin. Born around 1162 on the steppes of Mongolia, he was the son of a minor clan chief who was poisoned by rivals when Temüjin was nine years old.

What followed was one of the most brutal childhoods imaginable. His clan abandoned him and his family to die on the steppe. His first wife was kidnapped. He was enslaved by a rival clan and forced to wear a wooden yoke around his neck.

He escaped. He survived. And he spent the next thirty years transforming himself from a fugitive into the ruler of all Mongolia.

By 1206, Temüjin had unified every nomadic tribe on the Mongolian steppe under his command. At a great assembly — a kurultai — he was proclaimed Genghis Khan: "Universal Ruler."

He was approximately 44 years old. And he was just getting started.

---

## [THE MONGOL WAR MACHINE — 3:00–5:30]

The Mongol army was unlike anything the world had faced.

The core of Mongol military power was the cavalry archer. Every Mongol warrior rode from birth — they learned to ride before they could walk. They could fire arrows with extraordinary accuracy while galloping at full speed. They could live off their horses — drinking fermented mare's milk, bleeding their horses for nutrition when necessary, riding 60 to 80 miles per day without supply lines.

The standard tactics were psychological as much as military.

First, the Mongols would offer a city a chance to surrender. If it surrendered, the population was generally spared — with heavy tribute extracted. If it chose to fight, the Mongols would signal their warriors: no mercy. Entire cities were put to the sword as a message to every city that came after.

It worked. Dozens of cities surrendered without a fight simply because of what they knew the Mongols would do if they didn't.

For cities that resisted, the Mongols had become masters of siege warfare — something unusual for nomadic cavalry. They conscripted Chinese engineers, Persian architects, and the best siege experts from every conquered civilization. They used catapults, siege towers, fire weapons, and — in one documented case — catapults loaded with plague-infected corpses.

---

## [THE CONQUESTS — 5:30–9:30]

Genghis Khan began with northern China. The Jin Dynasty — which controlled the most populous region on earth — fell after years of brutal warfare. Millions died.

He turned west. Central Asia — the Khwarazmian Empire, which had made the mistake of killing Mongol ambassadors — was annihilated with extraordinary thoroughness. Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv — cities that had existed for thousands of years and were among the most sophisticated in the world — were destroyed so completely that they never recovered their former glory. Merv, once the largest city in the world, had its population massacred. Population estimates suggest up to 700,000 dead in that single city.

Genghis Khan died in 1227. His successors continued the conquest.

His son Ögedei completed the destruction of the Jin Dynasty, then turned to Europe. In 1241, Mongol forces invaded Poland and Hungary simultaneously — defeating every European army they faced at the battles of Legnica and Mohi. They reached the Adriatic Sea. Vienna trembled.

Then, inexplicably, they turned back. Ögedei had died. The Mongol generals had to return to Mongolia to elect a new Khan.

Europe was saved — not by military force, but by internal Mongol politics.

The most devastating conquest came later, in 1258. Möngke Khan's brother Hulagu led a massive army into the Middle East. Baghdad — the capital of the Abbasid Caliphate, the cultural and intellectual center of the Islamic world, home to the legendary House of Wisdom library — fell after a brief siege.

What followed was the end of the Islamic Golden Age.

Hulagu's forces sacked Baghdad for weeks. The Caliph was wrapped in a carpet and trampled to death by horses — the Mongols believed it was taboo to spill royal blood on the ground. The House of Wisdom — one of the greatest repositories of human knowledge ever assembled, containing works of mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy from across the ancient world — was destroyed. The books were thrown into the Tigris River. According to legend, the water ran black with ink.

An estimated 200,000 to 800,000 people were killed in Baghdad.

The Mongol advance was only stopped the following year by the Mamluks of Egypt at the Battle of Ain Jalut — one of the very few forces that ever defeated the Mongols in open battle.

---

## [THE PAX MONGOLICA — 9:30–12:00]

Here is the counterintuitive part of the Mongol story.

Once conquest was complete, the Mongol Empire became something genuinely remarkable: the first unified trade network spanning from China to Eastern Europe.

The Pax Mongolica — the Mongol Peace — made the Silk Road safe for the first time in centuries. Merchants could travel from China to Persia without fear of bandits. Marco Polo's journey was made possible by Mongol protection. Technologies, ideas, and goods flowed across the continent at a rate never before seen.

The Mongols were almost entirely religiously tolerant — they had Buddhists, Muslims, Christians, and Shamanists all within their government. They cared about competence, not faith.

Chinese inventions — gunpowder, printing — spread westward through the Mongol network. Persian mathematics and astronomy moved east. The cross-pollination of ideas sparked by this forced integration played a role in ending the European Dark Ages.

The same empire that killed 40 million people also created the conditions for the Renaissance.

---

## [DECLINE — 12:00–13:30]

The Mongol Empire began to fragment within fifty years of its peak. The descendants of Genghis Khan fought each other for power. The vast territories broke into successor states — the Golden Horde in Russia, the Ilkhanate in Persia, the Yuan Dynasty in China.

These successor states gradually assimilated into the cultures they had conquered. The Yuan Dynasty became Chinese. The Ilkhanate converted to Islam. The Golden Horde's descendants became the ancestors of modern Kazakhstan and other Central Asian nations.

The Black Death — which spread along Mongol trade routes — killed a third of Europe's population in the mid-14th century, contributing to the final dissolution of Mongol power.

---

## [CLOSING — 13:30–15:00]

Genghis Khan has been called a monster. He has been called the greatest conqueror in history. He has been called the father of globalization.

He was probably all three.

What he built — through a combination of extraordinary military genius and unrestrained violence — was the first true global empire. And its collapse, paradoxically, scattered the knowledge it had accumulated across the world.

The destruction of Baghdad may have ended the Islamic Golden Age. But the trade networks the Mongols created helped spark the Renaissance in Europe.

History rarely offers clean moral conclusions. The Mongol Empire is evidence that civilization's greatest catastrophes and its greatest advances can come from the same source.
