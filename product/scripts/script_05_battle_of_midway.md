# Script 05: The Four Minutes That Won World War II
**Topic:** Battle of Midway, June 4–7, 1942  
**Estimated Duration:** 12–14 minutes

## [HOOK — 0:00–0:45]

Four minutes.

In four minutes, at 10:22 AM on June 4, 1942, dive bombers from the United States Navy turned the tide of World War II in the Pacific.

In those four minutes, three of Japan's four frontline aircraft carriers — the most powerful naval force in the Pacific — were reduced to burning wrecks.

In four minutes, the war that Japan thought it was winning became the war Japan would ultimately lose.

And it very nearly didn't happen at all.

---

## [CONTEXT — 0:45–2:30]

Six months earlier, Japan had delivered the most shocking military strike in American history: the attack on Pearl Harbor, December 7, 1941.

In two hours, Japanese naval aircraft had destroyed most of the American Pacific fleet's battleships. America was in a state of shock. The Pacific Ocean seemed to belong to Japan.

Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto — the architect of Pearl Harbor — understood one critical weakness in what Japan had achieved. The American aircraft carriers had not been at Pearl Harbor. They had survived. And Yamamoto knew that in this new era of naval warfare, carriers, not battleships, were the decisive weapon.

If America could rebuild its carrier forces, it could threaten Japan. Yamamoto needed to lure the American carriers into a decisive battle and destroy them before that happened.

He chose a small atoll 1,300 miles northwest of Hawaii: Midway Island.

Midway was a strategically vital communications base. Yamamoto calculated that America could not afford to let it fall — and would send whatever carriers it had to defend it.

He was right. What he didn't know was that America had already read his mail.

---

## [THE CODEBREAKERS — 2:30–4:30]

The most important weapon at the Battle of Midway was never fired.

American cryptanalysts at Station HYPO in Pearl Harbor had spent months cracking JN-25 — the Japanese Navy's operational code. By May 1942, they had achieved something extraordinary: they could partially decrypt Japanese naval communications.

The intelligence they gathered showed a massive Japanese operation being planned at a target designated "AF." But where was AF?

A brilliant young codebreaker named Joseph Rochefort devised an elegant trap. He had Midway radio a false message in the clear: "Midway's water distillation equipment has broken down."

Days later, an intercepted Japanese message reported that "AF is short of fresh water."

AF was Midway.

Admiral Chester Nimitz now knew where the Japanese were going, approximately when, and with what force. He had two carrier groups — around three carriers — against Japan's four. Outnumbered, but not surprised.

He positioned his forces in ambush, northeast of Midway, where Japanese reconnaissance planes would be unlikely to find them.

---

## [THE BATTLE — 4:30–9:30]

On the morning of June 4, the battle opened in chaos.

Japanese aircraft struck Midway Island, inflicting heavy damage but failing to put it out of operation. American air attacks on the Japanese fleet were a series of disasters — torpedo bomber squadrons were slaughtered, achieving almost nothing against the Japanese carriers. Japanese Zero fighters dominated the air.

Admiral Chuichi Nagumo, commanding the Japanese carrier force, made a critical and fatal decision. His carriers had been armed for a second strike on Midway — land bombs. But then reports came in of American ships — possibly carriers — to the northeast.

He ordered the bombs changed. All aircraft on the hangar decks would be re-armed with torpedoes for an attack on ships.

This process of rearming took time. And it created chaos on the hangar decks — fuel lines running across the floor, ordnance stacked in the corridors, aircraft partially armed.

Nagumo also needed to recover his first-wave aircraft — they were returning from Midway, low on fuel. He had to land them first.

For a critical window of time, the Japanese carriers were at their most vulnerable: decks full of aircraft, ordnance scattered below, fuel lines exposed.

And that is the moment the American dive bombers arrived.

---

## [THE FOUR MINUTES — 9:30–11:30]

Torpedo Bomber Squadron 8 — VT-8 — had attacked the Japanese carriers earlier that morning and been annihilated. Of 15 aircraft, only one pilot survived. Ensign George Gay, floating in the ocean beneath a seat cushion, watched what happened next.

Their deaths had not been wasted. The Zeros that slaughtered VT-8 had been drawn down to sea level. When dive bombers from USS Enterprise and USS Yorktown arrived at altitude, there were almost no fighters to intercept them.

Lieutenant Commander Clarence "Wade" McClusky led the Enterprise's dive bombers. He had been flying a search pattern, running low on fuel, when he spotted the wake of a Japanese destroyer and followed it directly to the Japanese fleet.

At 10:22 AM, McClusky's bombers rolled into their dives.

Three carriers — Akagi, Kaga, and Soryu — were hit almost simultaneously. The bombs penetrated to the hangar decks and detonated amid the scattered fuel and ordnance. The fires were immediate and uncontrollable.

Akagi. Kaga. Soryu. All burning.

It took four minutes.

That afternoon, Yorktown's aircraft found and fatally damaged the fourth Japanese carrier, Hiryu, before Hiryu's aircraft crippled and later sank Yorktown.

By the end of June 5, all four Japanese carriers were gone.

---

## [AFTERMATH — 11:30–13:00]

Japan lost four fleet carriers — the same four that had attacked Pearl Harbor. They lost over 3,000 men and the cream of their carrier aviation: experienced, irreplaceable pilots.

America had lost one carrier and 307 men. A painful price, but a fraction of Japan's.

Japan would build new carriers. But it could never replace the experienced pilots it lost at Midway. American industry, fully mobilized, would eventually produce carriers faster than Japan could sink them.

Six months after Pearl Harbor had left America seemingly helpless in the Pacific, Midway had fundamentally changed the strategic situation. Japan was now on the defensive.

The road from Midway to Japan's surrender in August 1945 was still three brutal years long — Guadalcanal, Iwo Jima, Okinawa lay ahead. But the outcome was no longer in doubt.

---

## [CLOSING — 13:00–14:00]

The Battle of Midway was decided by intelligence, by luck, and by four minutes of precision bombing.

It was decided by codebreakers working in a basement in Pearl Harbor. By a pilot who followed a destroyer's wake. By a Japanese admiral who made one wrong decision at the worst possible moment.

And by the torpedo bomber crews who flew into annihilation without knowing they were clearing the sky for the aircraft that would win the battle.

History is often made by four minutes that only happen because of everything that came before them.
