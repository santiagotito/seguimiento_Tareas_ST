## [HOOK — 0:00–0:45]

Four minutes.

In four minutes, at 10:22 AM on June 4, 1942, dive bombers from the United States Navy turned the tide of World War II in the Pacific.

In those four minutes, three of Japan's four frontline aircraft carriers — the most powerful naval force in the Pacific — were reduced to burning wrecks.

In four minutes, the war that Japan thought it was winning became the war Japan would ultimately lose.

And it very nearly didn't happen at all.

---

## [CONTEXTO — 0:45–2:30]

Six months earlier, Japan had delivered the most shocking military strike in American history: Pearl Harbor, December 7, 1941.

In two hours, Japanese naval aircraft had destroyed most of the Pacific fleet's battleships. America was in shock. The Pacific Ocean seemed to belong to Japan.

Admiral Yamamoto — the architect of Pearl Harbor — understood one critical weakness: the American aircraft carriers hadn't been at Pearl Harbor. They had survived. And in this new era of naval warfare, carriers were the decisive weapon.

He chose a small atoll 1,300 miles northwest of Hawaii: Midway Island.

Yamamoto calculated that America could not afford to let it fall. He was right. What he didn't know was that America had already read his mail.

---

## [LOS CRIPTOANALISTAS — 2:30–4:30]

The most important weapon at Midway was never fired.

American cryptanalysts at Pearl Harbor had partially cracked JN-25 — the Japanese Navy's operational code. They knew a massive operation was being planned at target "AF." But where was AF?

Codebreaker Joseph Rochefort devised an elegant trap. He had Midway radio a false message in the clear: "Midway's water distillation equipment has broken down."

Days later, an intercepted Japanese message reported that "AF is short of fresh water."

AF was Midway.

Admiral Chester Nimitz now knew where, approximately when, and with what force. He positioned his carriers — three vs Japan's four — in ambush northeast of Midway, where Japanese reconnaissance would be unlikely to find them.

---

## [LA BATALLA — 4:30–9:30]

On the morning of June 4, Japanese aircraft struck Midway Island, inflicting heavy damage. American air attacks on the Japanese fleet were disasters — torpedo bomber squadrons were slaughtered, achieving almost nothing.

Admiral Nagumo made a critical and fatal decision. His carriers had been armed for a second strike on Midway — land bombs. Then reports came in of American ships to the northeast.

He ordered the bombs changed to torpedoes. This process created chaos on hangar decks — fuel lines running across the floor, ordnance stacked in corridors.

He also needed to recover his first-wave aircraft, low on fuel.

For a critical window, the Japanese carriers were at their most vulnerable: decks full of aircraft, ordnance scattered below, fuel lines exposed.

That is the exact moment the American dive bombers arrived.

---

## [LOS CUATRO MINUTOS — 9:30–11:30]

Torpedo Bomber Squadron 8 had attacked earlier and been annihilated — 15 aircraft, only one survivor. Ensign George Gay, floating beneath a seat cushion, watched what came next.

Their deaths had not been wasted. The Zeros that slaughtered them had been drawn down to sea level. When dive bombers arrived at altitude, there were almost no fighters to intercept them.

Lieutenant Commander McClusky had been flying a search pattern, nearly out of fuel, when he spotted the wake of a Japanese destroyer and followed it directly to the fleet.

At 10:22 AM, his bombers rolled into their dives.

Three carriers — Akagi, Kaga, and Soryu — were hit almost simultaneously. Bombs penetrated to hangar decks and detonated amid scattered fuel and ordnance. The fires were immediate and uncontrollable.

Akagi. Kaga. Soryu. All burning.

Four minutes.

That afternoon, Yorktown's aircraft fatally damaged the fourth carrier, Hiryu — before Hiryu's aircraft crippled and later sank Yorktown.

By June 5, all four Japanese carriers were gone.

---

## [CONSECUENCIAS — 11:30–13:00]

Japan lost four fleet carriers. Over 3,000 men. The cream of their carrier aviation — experienced, irreplaceable pilots.

America lost one carrier and 307 men.

Japan would build new carriers. But it could never replace those pilots. American industry would eventually produce carriers faster than Japan could sink them.

Six months after Pearl Harbor had left America seemingly helpless, Midway had fundamentally changed the strategic situation. Japan was now on the defensive.

The road to Japan's surrender was still three brutal years long — Guadalcanal, Iwo Jima, Okinawa lay ahead. But the outcome was no longer in doubt.

---

## [CIERRE — 13:00–14:00]

Midway was decided by intelligence, by luck, and by four minutes of precision bombing.

By codebreakers in a basement. By a pilot who followed a destroyer's wake. By a Japanese admiral who made one wrong decision at the worst possible moment.

And by the torpedo bomber crews who flew into annihilation without knowing they were clearing the sky for the aircraft that would win the battle.

History is often made by four minutes that only happen because of everything that came before them.
