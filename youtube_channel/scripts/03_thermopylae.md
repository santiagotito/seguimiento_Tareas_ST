## [HOOK — 0:00–0:45]

Three hundred men. Against an army of hundreds of thousands.

They knew they were going to die. They chose to go anyway.

Here is the real story. It's more incredible than any movie.

---

## [CONTEXTO — 0:45–2:30]

In 480 BC, the Persian Empire was the largest empire the world had ever seen. Under Xerxes I — son of Darius the Great — it stretched from Egypt to India.

He wanted Greece.

Specifically, he wanted revenge. A decade earlier, his father Darius had sent an army that was defeated at Marathon. Xerxes assembled the largest invasion force the ancient world had ever seen — modern estimates suggest 200,000 to 300,000 men.

The Greek city-states formed a coalition. But they needed time. They needed a place to make a stand.

---

## [THERMOPYLAE — 2:30–4:00]

The strategist Themistocles identified the answer: a narrow coastal pass called Thermopylae — "Hot Gates" — flanked by steep cliffs and the sea.

At its narrowest point, the pass was barely fifteen meters wide. No matter how many soldiers Xerxes had, only a small number could push through at a time.

At Thermopylae, the Persians' numbers became irrelevant.

The Greeks sent approximately 7,000 soldiers to hold the pass. Among them, at the absolute front: 300 Spartan warriors commanded by King Leonidas I.

---

## [LOS ESPARTANOS — 4:00–6:00]

Spartan boys were taken from their families at age seven and placed in the agoge — a brutal state-run training system. They were underfed to teach them to forage. They were beaten — sometimes to death. They trained constantly for twenty-three years.

The result was something close to a human weapon.

Spartans fought in a formation called the phalanx — interlocking shields creating a wall nearly impossible to break. Every man trusted every other man completely.

Leonidas chose his 300 carefully. He selected only men who already had sons — men whose family line would continue even if they died. He knew from the start this was a suicide mission.

---

## [LA BATALLA — 6:00–10:00]

For two days, the Spartans and their allies held the pass.

The Persians sent wave after wave — the Medes, the Cissians, the Immortals, Xerxes' elite personal guard of 10,000. Each wave broke against the Greek phalanx.

Persian soldiers were individually brave, but carried shorter spears and smaller shields. The narrow pass prevented them from using their numerical advantage.

Xerxes reportedly rose from his throne three times in fear when he saw his Immortals driven back.

On the evening of the second day, a local Greek traitor — Ephialtes — showed Xerxes a mountain path around the pass.

When Leonidas received the news the next morning, the situation was clear. Surrounded within hours.

He sent most of the Greek force away. He kept the Spartans. And roughly 700 Thespians and 400 Thebans who chose to stay.

The final battle was fought with the knowledge that there was no escape.

The Spartans advanced onto wider ground, driving deep into the Persian formation to kill as many as possible. They broke their spears and fought with swords. When the swords broke, they fought with hands and teeth.

Leonidas was killed. The Persians flooded the battlefield four times to recover his body.

The final survivors were surrounded on a small hill. Arrows rained into them until nothing remained standing.

---

## [CONSECUENCIAS — 10:00–12:30]

The Persians burned Athens. It appeared Greece had fallen.

But Thermopylae had bought critical time.

Two months later, the Greek fleet met the Persian fleet at Salamis — trapped in narrow straits, the Persian advantage was nullified. The Persian fleet was destroyed.

Xerxes retreated. His land army was defeated the following year at Plataea. The Persian invasion was over.

Greece survived. And from Greece came democracy, philosophy, mathematics, theater, and the scientific method.

The epitaph at Thermopylae, by the poet Simonides:

*"Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie."*

---

## [CIERRE — 12:30–14:00]

Three hundred men chose to die so that something they loved might live.

What they loved — the idea of free people governing themselves — did live.

Two and a half thousand years later, it still does.
