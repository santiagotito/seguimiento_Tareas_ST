## [HOOK — 0:00–0:45]

Ten percent.

That's how much of the entire human population on earth was killed by one man and the armies he unleashed.

In the 13th century, Genghis Khan and his successors killed an estimated 40 million people. In some regions they conquered, the population didn't recover for centuries.

But here's the part nobody tells you: they also created the largest empire in human history, enabled the first global trade network, and sparked a cultural exchange that helped end the Dark Ages.

The Mongols were simultaneously the most destructive and one of the most transformative forces in human history.

---

## [GENGHIS KHAN — 0:45–3:00]

His name at birth was Temüjin. Born around 1162 on the steppes of Mongolia, son of a minor clan chief poisoned by rivals when Temüjin was nine years old.

What followed was one of the most brutal childhoods imaginable. His clan abandoned him. His first wife was kidnapped. He was enslaved and forced to wear a wooden yoke around his neck.

He escaped. He survived. He spent the next thirty years unifying every nomadic tribe on the Mongolian steppe.

By 1206, he was proclaimed Genghis Khan: "Universal Ruler." He was approximately 44 years old.

---

## [LA MÁQUINA DE GUERRA MONGOL — 3:00–5:30]

The Mongol army was unlike anything the world had faced.

Every Mongol warrior rode from birth — they learned to ride before they could walk. They could fire arrows with extraordinary accuracy while galloping at full speed. They could ride 60 to 80 miles per day without supply lines, living off fermented mare's milk.

Standard tactics were psychological as much as military: offer surrender, spare those who comply. If a city fights back — no mercy. Entire cities put to the sword as a message to every city that followed.

It worked. Dozens of cities surrendered without a fight.

For those that resisted, the Mongols had mastered siege warfare — conscripting Chinese engineers, Persian architects, the best siege experts from every conquered civilization. In one documented case: catapults loaded with plague-infected corpses.

---

## [LAS CONQUISTAS — 5:30–9:30]

Genghis Khan began with northern China. The Jin Dynasty fell after years of brutal warfare. Millions died.

He turned west. The Khwarazmian Empire — which had killed Mongol ambassadors — was annihilated. Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv — cities that had existed for thousands of years — were destroyed completely. Merv, once the largest city in the world, had its population massacred. Up to 700,000 dead in a single city.

After Genghis Khan's death in 1227, his successors continued.

In 1241, Mongol forces invaded Poland and Hungary simultaneously — defeating every European army they faced. They reached the Adriatic Sea. Vienna trembled.

Then, inexplicably, they turned back. Ögedei Khan had died. The generals had to return to elect a new Khan.

Europe was saved — not by military force, but by Mongol internal politics.

The most devastating conquest came in 1258. Baghdad — capital of the Abbasid Caliphate, the cultural and intellectual center of the Islamic world, home to the legendary House of Wisdom — fell.

The House of Wisdom — containing works of mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy from across the ancient world — was destroyed. The books were thrown into the Tigris River. According to legend, the water ran black with ink.

An estimated 200,000 to 800,000 people were killed in Baghdad.

The Mongol advance was stopped only by the Mamluks of Egypt at the Battle of Ain Jalut — one of the very few forces that ever defeated the Mongols in open battle.

---

## [LA PAX MONGOLICA — 9:30–12:00]

Here is the counterintuitive part.

Once conquest was complete, the Mongol Empire became the first unified trade network spanning from China to Eastern Europe.

The Pax Mongolica — the Mongol Peace — made the Silk Road safe for the first time in centuries. Marco Polo's journey was made possible by Mongol protection. Technologies, ideas, and goods flowed across the continent at unprecedented rates.

The Mongols were almost entirely religiously tolerant — Buddhists, Muslims, Christians, and Shamanists all worked within their government.

Chinese inventions — gunpowder, printing — spread westward. Persian mathematics moved east. This cross-pollination of ideas played a role in ending the European Dark Ages.

The same empire that killed 40 million people also created the conditions for the Renaissance.

---

## [DECLIVE — 12:00–13:30]

The Mongol Empire began to fragment within fifty years of its peak. Descendants of Genghis Khan fought each other for power. The vast territories broke into successor states.

The Black Death — which spread along Mongol trade routes — killed a third of Europe's population in the mid-14th century, contributing to the final dissolution of Mongol power.

---

## [CIERRE — 13:30–15:00]

Genghis Khan has been called a monster. He has been called the greatest conqueror in history. He has been called the father of globalization.

He was probably all three.

History rarely offers clean moral conclusions. The Mongol Empire is evidence that civilization's greatest catastrophes and its greatest advances can come from the same source.
