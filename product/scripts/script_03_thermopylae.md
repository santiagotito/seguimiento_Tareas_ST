## [HOOK — 0:00–0:45]

Three hundred men. Against an army of hundreds of thousands.

They knew they were going to die. They chose to go anyway.

And what they did at a narrow mountain pass called Thermopylae didn't just delay the Persian invasion. It bought enough time to save Greece — and with it, the entire idea of democracy.

Here is the real story. And it's more incredible than any movie.

---

## [CONTEXT — 0:45–2:30]

In 480 BC, the Persian Empire was the largest empire the world had ever seen. Under Xerxes I — son of Darius the Great — it stretched from Egypt to India, encompassing dozens of nations and hundreds of peoples. Xerxes commanded armies in the hundreds of thousands, a navy of over a thousand ships, and virtually unlimited resources.

He wanted Greece.

Specifically, he wanted revenge. A decade earlier, his father Darius had sent an army to punish the Greek city-states for supporting a revolt in Persian-controlled Ionia. That army had been defeated at Marathon — humiliatingly, by a much smaller Greek force.

Xerxes assembled the largest invasion force the ancient world had ever seen. Ancient sources — possibly exaggerated — claim over a million soldiers. Modern estimates suggest 200,000 to 300,000. Either way, Greece had never faced anything remotely like it.

The Greek city-states — perpetually squabbling, often at war with each other — somehow managed to form a coalition. But they needed time to prepare.

They needed a place to make a stand.

---

## [THERMOPYLAE — 2:30–4:00]

The strategist Themistocles identified the answer: a narrow coastal pass called Thermopylae — "Hot Gates" in Greek, named for the sulfur springs nearby.

At its narrowest point, the pass was barely fifteen meters wide — flanked on one side by steep cliffs, on the other by the sea. No matter how many soldiers Xerxes had, he could only send a small number through at a time.

At Thermopylae, the Persians' numbers became irrelevant.

The Greeks sent approximately 7,000 soldiers to hold the pass — drawn from multiple city-states. Among them, at the absolute front, were 300 Spartan warriors commanded by King Leonidas I.

---

## [THE SPARTANS — 4:00–6:00]

To understand what made the Spartans extraordinary, you need to understand their culture.

Spartan boys were taken from their families at age seven and placed in the agoge — a brutal state-run training system designed to produce the perfect soldier. They were underfed to teach them to forage. They were beaten — sometimes to death, to eliminate the weak. They trained constantly in combat, endurance, and tactical thinking. By the time a Spartan warrior reached adulthood at age 30, he had been training for twenty-three years.

The result was something close to a human weapon.

Spartan warriors didn't fight like other ancient soldiers. They didn't rely on individual heroics. They fought as a unit — in a formation called the phalanx, interlocking their shields to create a wall that was almost impossible to break. Every man trusted every other man completely.

Leonidas chose his 300 carefully. He selected only men who already had sons — men whose family line would continue even if they died. He knew from the start that this was a suicide mission.

---

## [THE BATTLE — 6:00–10:00]

For two days, the Spartans and their Greek allies held the pass.

The Persians sent wave after wave of their best troops — the Medes, the Cissians, the Immortals, Xerxes' elite personal guard of 10,000 supposedly "immortal" warriors. Each wave broke against the Greek phalanx.

The Persian soldiers were individually brave, but they carried shorter spears and smaller shields than the Greeks, and the narrow pass prevented them from using their numerical advantage. The Greeks could rotate fresh units in and out while the Persians fought in confined conditions against a wall of shields and spears.

Xerxes reportedly watched from a throne set on a hillside above the battle. Ancient sources say he rose from his seat three times in fear when he saw his Immortals driven back.

On the evening of the second day, a local Greek traitor — a man named Ephialtes — approached the Persian camp and offered to show Xerxes a mountain path that ran around the pass and came out behind the Greek position.

When Leonidas received the news the next morning, the situation was clear. They were going to be surrounded within hours.

He sent most of the Greek force away. He knew they would be needed for the battles to come.

He kept the Spartans. And roughly 700 Thespians and 400 Thebans who chose to stay.

The final battle was fought with the knowledge that there was no escape.

The Spartans advanced from the pass onto the wider ground beyond, driving deep into the Persian formation to kill as many as possible before they were overwhelmed. They broke their spears and fought with swords. When the swords broke, they fought with their hands and teeth.

Leonidas was killed. The Persians, according to Herodotus, flooded the battlefield four times to recover his body — Xerxes had been humiliated enough that he personally wanted to mutilate the Spartan king's corpse.

The final survivors were surrounded on a small hill. The Persians rained arrows into them until nothing remained standing.

---

## [AFTERMATH — 10:00–12:30]

The Persians burned Athens to the ground. It appeared that Greece had fallen.

But the stand at Thermopylae had bought critical time — time for the Athenian fleet to prepare at Salamis.

Two months after Thermopylae, the Greek fleet met the Persian fleet in the narrow straits of Salamis. Trapped in confined waters that nullified their numerical advantage — the same principle Leonidas had used at the pass — the Persian fleet was destroyed.

Xerxes watched from a golden throne on the shore as his armada was annihilated.

He retreated to Persia, leaving a land army behind. That army was defeated the following year at Plataea. The Persian invasion was over.

Greece survived. And from Greece — from Athens specifically — came democracy, philosophy, mathematics, theater, and the scientific method. The foundations of the civilization you live in today.

The epitaph carved at Thermopylae, attributed to the poet Simonides, is among the most famous sentences ever written:

*"Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie."*

---

## [CLOSING — 12:30–14:00]

There is a question historians still debate: if Thermopylae had not happened — if Greece had fallen in 480 BC — would the Western world exist as we know it?

Democracy, philosophy, science — these things developed in a very specific cultural environment. That environment required freedom to survive.

Would it have survived Persian conquest? Perhaps. History doesn't offer clean counterfactuals.

What we know is that three hundred men chose to die so that something they loved might live. And what they loved — the idea of free people governing themselves — did live.

Two and a half thousand years later, it still does.
